# B2C Analysis - selling to consumers

B2C means an individual buys for themselves. Defining features: many small customers, emotional + impulse-driven decisions, short consideration, brand and distribution dominate, and margins are usually thinner. The whole game is *acquisition cost vs. how much a customer is worth* - at scale.

## 1. The customer & the job

- **Who, specifically?** Not "everyone." Demographics matter less than the **job-to-be-done** and the moment of need. "Busy parents who want a healthy dinner with zero planning at 6pm on a weekday" beats "ages 25-45."
- **Why do they buy?** Consumer purchases are emotional, then rationalized: status, convenience, identity, fear, belonging, pleasure, saving time. Name the real driver.
- **Frequency & habit.** Is this a one-time buy, occasional, or habitual? Habitual/subscription products are far more valuable because retention compounds. A one-and-done product must win on constant new acquisition - much harder.

## 2. The economics (this kills most B2C ideas)

B2C lives or dies on the gap between **what a customer is worth** and **what it costs to get them** - at scale, after the easy early adopters are gone.

- **CAC** - what it actually costs to acquire one paying customer through your real channels (paid ads, influencer, SEO, referral). Early "free" customers (friends, launch buzz) lie to you; model the *marginal* CAC once you're buying growth.
- **LTV** - purchase value x gross margin x repeat frequency x lifespan. For one-time products, LTV is basically one purchase's margin - brutal.
- **LTV:CAC >= 3:1**, same rule. Most failed DTC brands had LTV:CAC near 1 because ad costs rose and repeat purchase didn't materialize.
- **Contribution margin per unit** - after COGS, shipping, payment fees, returns. Physical-goods B2C often has 20-40% margin, which leaves little room for CAC. Digital products have high margin but high competition.
- **Payback** - ideally recover CAC on the *first* purchase for one-time products; subscriptions can pay back over a few months.

If the user is selling a one-time physical product with 30% margin and Instagram CAC is climbing, say it directly: the math is hostile, and the answer is either repeat purchase, higher AOV, or a cheaper channel.

## 3. Acquisition channels (the make-or-break)

B2C is a distribution game. Rank the realistic channels and their honest state:

- **Paid (Meta/Google/TikTok)** - fast but increasingly expensive; works only if LTV is high or margin is fat.
- **Organic social / content** - slow, compounding, cheap; the user's note suggests they prefer organic - this is a real edge if they can sustain it.
- **SEO** - high-intent, durable, slow to build.
- **Referral / virality** - only some products are inherently shareable; don't assume virality, engineer it or don't count on it.
- **Marketplaces / app stores** - instant distribution, but you rent the customer and pay a tax.
- **Influencer / community** - strong for identity-driven products.

The killer question: **is there at least one channel where you can acquire customers profitably and repeatably?** If acquisition only works while you're personally hustling, it's a hobby, not a scalable business - flag this.

## 4. Retention & brand

- **Retention curve** - what % come back? For subscriptions, watch month-1 and month-3 churn. A retention curve that flattens above zero = a real business; one that decays to zero = a leaky bucket no amount of acquisition fixes.
- **Brand** - in B2C with low switching costs, brand *is* the moat. Why would someone choose and re-choose you over a cheaper copy? If the only answer is "we were first" or "lower price," that's fragile.

## 5. Competitive & market

- B2C markets are usually crowded and low-barrier. What stops a competitor copying you next week? Often: brand, community, supply-chain/cost advantage, or distribution lock-in.
- Is the market growing, saturated, or a fad? Distinguish a durable behavior shift from a trend you'd be late to.
