# New Business / Idea Validation

A new venture has no performance history, so you can't analyze results  -  you analyze **the strength of the bet**. The job is to find the assumptions the whole thing rests on and judge how much evidence supports them. Most ideas die from one of a small number of fatal flaws; find them before the user spends a year.

## The order matters  -  check the fatal flaws first

Don't polish a logo for a business with no buyer. Evaluate in this order and stop to flag any hard "no":

### 1. Is there a real problem, felt by a real person, right now?
- Vitamin vs. painkiller: do people actively seek a solution, or is it "nice to have"? Painkillers sell.
- Who *specifically* has this problem badly enough to pay? If the answer is "everyone," that's a red flag  -  narrow it.
- Evidence test: has the user talked to people with this problem who weren't friends? Have those people *paid for a worse solution already*? Existing spend is the strongest demand signal there is.

### 2. Is there a buyer, and will they actually pay (and how much)?
- Separate *user* from *payer* (especially relevant in B2B and in "free app" models).
- Willingness to pay is a number, not a vibe. The only real validation is someone pre-ordering, signing an LOI, or paying. "People said they'd use it" is nearly worthless.

### 3. Is the market big enough *for the user's goals*?
- TAM/SAM/SOM, but honestly: Total Addressable, Serviceable Addressable, and the slice you could realistically *Obtain* in a few years.
- A lifestyle business needs a much smaller market than a venture-scale one. Match market size to *their* ambition  -  a $2M/yr niche is a great solo business and a terrible VC bet.
- Build TAM bottom-up (customers x price), not top-down ("1% of a $10B market").

### 4. Can *this* person/team actually build and sell it?
- Founder-market fit: do they have an unfair advantage here  -  skills, domain knowledge, distribution, audience? (E.g. a developer who is *also* a specialty-coffee producer has a rare, real edge in coffee-tech.)
- What's the hardest part  -  the tech, the distribution, or the operations  -  and can they do it with the resources they have?

### 5. Why now, and why won't this get crushed?
- **Why now:** what changed (tech, behavior, regulation, cost) that makes this newly possible? If nothing changed, why doesn't it already exist / why did past attempts fail?
- **Defensibility:** even if it works, what stops a competitor or an incumbent from copying it once you've proven the market? Early traction is not a moat.

## Lightweight Business Model Canvas

For a new idea, sketch the nine blocks quickly to expose gaps  -  the empty boxes are the risks:

1. **Value proposition**  -  the one sentence: for [who], we solve [problem] by [how], unlike [alternative].
2. **Customer segments**  -  who, sharply defined.
3. **Channels**  -  how they discover and buy.
4. **Customer relationships**  -  self-serve, high-touch, community?
5. **Revenue streams**  -  how money is made; one-time, subscription, usage, marketplace take.
6. **Cost structure**  -  the big costs; where the money goes.
7. **Key resources**  -  what you must have (tech, capital, people, IP).
8. **Key activities**  -  what the business must do well.
9. **Key partners**  -  who you depend on.

The gap between "Revenue streams" and "Cost structure" at scale is the business. If you can't see how it's positive, that's the analysis.

## Output template

```
# Validation: [Idea]
*Assumptions: ...*

## The bet in one sentence
[for X, solve Y by Z, unlike W]

## Fatal-flaw check
1. Real problem? [evidence / verdict]
2. Buyer & willingness to pay? [evidence / verdict]
3. Market big enough for the goal? [bottom-up estimate]
4. Founder-market fit? [the unfair advantage, honestly]
5. Why now & defensibility? [verdict]

## Riskiest assumption
[the single thing most likely to be false that the whole idea depends on]

## Verdict & cheapest possible test
[go / no-go / not-yet + the ONE experiment that would most cheaply
prove or kill the riskiest assumption in the next 2 weeks  -  almost always
"talk to / try to pre-sell to 5-10 real potential buyers," not "build the product"]
```

The most valuable thing you can do for someone with a new idea is identify the riskiest assumption and the cheapest test of it  -  *before* they build anything.
