# Competitive Analysis

The goal is to understand the structure of the competition well enough to find a defensible position  -  or to conclude there isn't one. Use web search to get *current* competitors and *real* prices (`research.md`); a competitive analysis built from memory is worthless.

## 1. Map the real competitive set

List who the customer actually chooses between  -  three layers, and don't skip the last two:

- **Direct**  -  same solution, same buyer (the obvious rivals).
- **Indirect / substitutes**  -  a different solution to the same job (e.g. for a scheduling app: a spreadsheet, a VA, pen-and-paper).
- **Status quo / "do nothing"**  -  usually the biggest competitor of all, especially for new products. People are lazy and busy; inertia wins by default.

## 2. Competitor teardown

For each meaningful competitor, capture (from their site/pricing/reviews):

- **Target segment**  -  who they're really for.
- **Positioning**  -  the one thing they claim.
- **Pricing**  -  actual current numbers and model.
- **Strengths**  -  why customers pick them.
- **Weaknesses**  -  what customers complain about (reviews, forums)  -  this is where your opening is.

A small table works well. The pattern you're hunting for: an underserved segment, or a need everyone handles badly.

## 3. Porter's Five Forces  -  industry structure

This tells you whether the *industry* is even attractive, separate from your specific idea. Assess each as high/medium/low and *why*:

- **Competitive rivalry**  -  many similar players fighting on price = brutal margins. Few differentiated players = healthier.
- **Threat of new entrants**  -  low barriers (easy to build/launch) means today's advantage erodes fast. What raises barriers: capital, regulation, network effects, brand, proprietary data.
- **Threat of substitutes**  -  can the job be done another, cheaper way? High substitutes cap your pricing power.
- **Buyer power**  -  few large buyers (common in B2B) can squeeze you; many small buyers (B2C) have less individual leverage but are fickle.
- **Supplier power**  -  dependence on a key supplier, platform, or API that can raise prices or cut you off (e.g. one ad platform, one payment processor, one app store).

The output isn't five labels  -  it's the 1-2 forces that most shape this market's profitability and what they imply for the strategy.

## 4. Positioning map

Pick the **two axes that actually drive customer choice** in this category (not generic price/quality unless those truly are the axes  -  e.g. "self-serve <-> full-service" vs. "SMB <-> enterprise"). Plot the competitors. The empty quadrant with real demand is the white space. If every quadrant is crowded, that's a finding: you'll compete on execution/brand, not position.

## 5. Defensibility

Even if you find a gap, ask: once you prove the market, what stops a competitor (or an incumbent with distribution) from copying you? Real moats: switching costs, network/data effects, brand, regulation/certifications, proprietary distribution, structural cost advantage. "First mover" and "we work harder" are not moats.

## Output template

```
# Competitive Analysis: [Business / Market], [Country]

## Competitive set

[the 1-2 dominant forces + implication; brief note on the rest]

## Positioning & white space
[the two real axes; where competitors sit; the gap (or "no clean gap")]

## Your defensible position (or lack of one)
[the angle + the moat that would protect it  -  honestly]

## Data & sources

```
